# Let the topic pick the script architecture

The ten channels don't run ten different formulas. Their scripts fall into **seven architectures**, and two choices separate them: where the explanation sits relative to the story, and what drives the viewer forward. That driver can be a chain of problems, a clock, a mystery, a cost or a thesis. Veritasium and Kurzgesagt run **problem chains**, spending about 70–80% of runtime on "this works, but it breaks here, so they did that". Mentour Pilot and Scott Manley's failure explainers run **primer-before-failure**: 20–52% of runtime explains the parts before the event, and the event lands at 65–79% of runtime in Mentour. Air Crash Investigation (ACI) runs a **detective investigation**. It shows the crash early, spends about 58% of the episode cycling through suspects, and closes the central question at 87–94%. The B1M runs **scale tension**, with a hook of about 7%, a sponsor mid-roll at about 23% and the title's promise paid at 59–65%. India in Pixels runs **question-to-opinion**: numbered reasons, the answer at 61–81% and a flagged personal take at about 88%. The Infographics Show runs **timeline scenarios** at 203–216 words a minute, with 65–92% of runtime in timestamped event narration. The Culture Mafia and the news roundups of Marcus House and Manley run **segmented analysis**, where chapters do the pacing instead of suspense. For your faceless tech-concepts channel, five beat sheets cover the four topic types. Use a problem chain for concept explainers. Use primer-before-failure for an incident whose root cause is known, and the detective structure for one whose cause was hunted or disputed. Use scale tension with numbered mechanisms for "how big system X works", and a timeline scenario with a reality check for "what if X failed". The evidence is ten channels' caption tracks: 4 to 10 whole videos per architecture, no retention data, and several tracks that are auto-captioned, machine-translated or translated from Hindi. Treat every percentage below as a band to test, not a norm.

Three patterns run across every architecture. First, **no channel answers its title question early**. Kurzgesagt's short videos are the exception that proves the rule: they answer at a quarter of the way in, then escalate or open a bigger question. The B1M, India in Pixels, ACI and Mentour all hold the title's payoff until 60% or later. Second, **the hook is short only where the title already carries the stakes**. Veritasium uses 1–2% and Mentour about 1%, while the B1M spends 5–9% because its hook does scale-setting work too. Third, **the sponsor goes where it costs the least suspense**. That is after the payoff for Veritasium, at a lull inside the primer for Mentour, about a quarter in for the B1M, and on a tease at ten minutes for House. None of the ten puts its main read inside the last-act payoff.

## Problem chains spend three quarters of runtime on "but, therefore"

**Veritasium** scripts share one spine across three very different topics. A 1–2% hook is followed by a 9–15% first concrete model, then a 72–80% chain of problem, partial answer and new problem, a 3–6% take, and a sponsor read placed after the ending. In "Future Computers Will Be Radically Different" the chain runs from analog's drawbacks to the perceptron's limits, then to matrix multiplication, data and GPUs, and finally to the energy and memory limits that make "the perfect storm for analog computers" at 14:26 of 21:42 ([Analog](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GVsUOuSjvcg)). The loop planted in the first 12 seconds closes at 66%. A drawback planted at 2:42 ("you can expect about a 1% error") comes back as a virtue at 14:41: "neural networks don't need the precision of digital computers." **The mechanism never runs as one block longer than about 215 seconds.** Each block is broken by a story beat, a "but" or a re-hook. Story carries the explanation rather than sitting beside it: in the Markov video, four mechanisms are each attached to a different person and decade, and "Little did he know…" handoffs announce the next story before the current one ends ([Markov](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZeIEiBrT_w)). The ending is a callback in all three videos: Rosenblatt's digital computer, the Erdős quote repeated "with a new meaning", and the card-shuffle question answered 31 minutes after it was asked, with the sponsor read placed between the question and its answer ([Collatz](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=094y1Z2wpJg)). Participation prompts ("Pick a number, any number. Seven?") appear in two of the three.

**Kurzgesagt's** 2015–2019 videos compress the same logic into 6–7 minutes with almost no people in them. A 4–9% hook is followed by a 14–19% primer and about 70% of short beats, each opened by a one-line signpost. "To understand why this is a problem, we have to clear up some basics" opens the Quantum primer, which climbs from chip to gate to transistor at one fact per sentence ([Quantum](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JhHMJCUmq28)). Black Holes answers its two hook questions in order, at 23% and 45%, and admits the edge of knowledge: "Right now, we don't know what happens next" ([Black Holes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e-P5IFTqB98)). Two of the three end on an open question on purpose. The nuclear what-if shows a second Kurzgesagt mode, the **escalation ladder**. It runs one scenario, gives a mild verdict ("human life will go on"), then asks "OK, but what if we explode more nuclear weapons?" and runs a bigger one with the same "Three, two, one" trigger ([Nukes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JyECrGp-Sw8)). The 2024 AI video stretches this into 16:43. It spends 18% on a history-of-intelligence primer before the subject appears, then climbs narrow, general and super rungs, each entered with "but what if". Only auto-captions exist for it, so treat that shape as a hypothesis ([AI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fa8k8IQ1_X0)).

What a problem chain needs, and what makes it fit your concept explainers, is a sequence of designs, each of which fixes the last one's failure. Passwords (plain text, then hashes, salts and slow hashes), caching, consensus and compression all have that shape. Concepts with one mechanism and no history of failure don't, and forcing them into a chain produces filler.

## Primer-before-failure lets the viewer watch the crash coming

**Mentour Pilot** tells a chronological story and keeps pausing it to explain things. The failure sits in the last 25–45% of runtime: the event starts at **65% (El Al 1862), 76% (Bering Air 445) and 71% (Tenerife)** ([El Al](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LT2F4PUlDnI), [Bering](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5axb7MVMyrI), [Tenerife](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2d9B9RN5quA)). In El Al, a hidden-flaw line at 3:47 ("a component holding up one of the aircraft's four engines was already carrying within it a terrible flaw") opens a **24-minute pause**. It covers the fuse-pin design, the failure order, the redesign history, fatigue, the inspection blind zones and the precedent accidents. The pause closes with "So, with all of this in mind, let's go back to El Al Flight 1862" at 28:39. The primer plants one fact on purpose: "And that sequence is worth remembering" (9:59). The event pays it off: "do you remember which pins were supposed to fail first in a scenario like this? Yeah" (29:41). Across five videos the averages are a 1% hook, 4% sponsor, 56% pre-event build, 26% event, 11% after and 1% CTA. The investigation after the event is short (5–16%) because the explanation was already delivered.

Bering spreads three primers so that each lands 0–3 minutes before the system it explains is tested: icing, then the TKS anti-ice system, then autopilot altitude hold. Tension comes from the clock, not from short sentences. Mentour's sentences average about 26 words through setup, primer and event, and drop to about 17 only in the last 80 seconds. Key seconds are stretched about sixfold. Concorde's "121 seconds" fill 12.5 minutes of video, and Tenerife's last 39 seconds fill about 4 minutes, ending in one sentence per second ([Concorde](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C-nALYF73hU)). Tenerife adds a "last chances" list: "there are still three things that could have potentially stopped it", named in order, then closed with "those were the last three things that could have stopped This Disaster". Blame is handled the same way every time. Uncertainty is flagged where it occurs ("investigators were never able to determine the exact sequence… with complete certainty"). Non-clues are labelled ("That wouldn't have an impact on what we're soon about to see"). The thesis is one sentence: El Al was "brought down by a chain of assumptions that all proved to be incomplete at the same time" (41:40).

**Scott Manley's** failure explainers share this architecture with a shorter clock. "Why Chernobyl Exploded" moves from a peg to a scope fence ("I'm not gonna name names"), then a 22% primer on chain reactions, moderators and the RBMK layout. The safety test follows, then the xenon complication, then two reveals (the positive void coefficient, then the AZ-5 scram's graphite tips), and finally the outcome and a take ([Chernobyl](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q3d3rzFTrLg)). The shutdown switch is planted as reassurance at ~12:30 and turned into the cause at ~16:30–18:00, the same move as Mentour's fuse pins. Across five explainers Manley's shares are a peg of 2–15%, a primer of 20–25%, a complication of 10–20%, a reveal of 25–35%, consequences of 15–25% and a take of 2–9%. He also has a portable way of building one technical point. He states the claim, names the mechanism, puts a number on it, checks the number against something familiar, then states the limit. In the Tesla video, 20 kN of cold-gas thrust is checked against how long gravity takes to get from zero to 60 (about 2.7 seconds), then against 40–50 kWh to recompress the gas, and finally against "None of the demonstrations lasted longer than 3 seconds" ([Tesla](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ex4Qg-gkLNg)). One difference from Mentour matters for a new channel. Manley's event comes earlier: the test begins at 28% and the reveal at 60%, so the viewer waits less for the payoff.

## Detective episodes show the crash early and spend 58% hunting

**ACI** inverts Mentour. The crash appears in the first 3–16% of the episode, as aftermath (Turkish Airlines 1951), as a brief staged event (United 585) or as a montage of fragments (EgyptAir 990). The explanation is delivered in installments, each triggered by a clue a named investigator chases. About **55–60% of each episode is investigation**: an evidence inventory, then suspect, test and eliminate, repeated. TK1951 cycles through the engines, a microburst and an air traffic control (ATC) "slam dunk" approach. It declares dead ends aloud ("investigators are stumped", "but none of this explains why flight 1951 crashed") and switches focus with "this find changes the focus of the investigation" ([TK1951](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C9EI9oIucSU)). Primers arrive only when a clue calls for them. The radio altimeter is explained at 13:12 after the clue that "one of the instruments… had the plane already on the ground". Autopilot versus autothrottle is explained at 24:56, after the engines turn out to have been at idle. The viewer sees the crash two or three times, each time with one more piece of explanation, until a minute-by-minute reconstruction at 31:56–38:02 closes the cold open's binary question ("the plane or… the pilots") at **87%**.

United 585 shows how far the structure stretches. The report comes back "undetermined" at 38%. Then "killer strikes again" brings in a second crash, and a third event with a living pilot finally breaks the case. The reveal lands at 42:03, and a line from the cold open is replayed at 44:52 ([UA585](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w68C05Rv6Sc)). EgyptAir 990 ends deliberately unresolved: the investigator's pride and a family member's "IT'S A COVER-UP" are placed back to back, closing on "UNANSWERED QUESTIONS" ([EgyptAir 990](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FFpo-Z3g8nQ)). ACI uses an analogy for every primer ("The PCU performs like a car's power steering", a valve "shaped like a soda can"). It puts judgements in experts' mouths, and it ends with layered blame: hardware, then crew, then an expert saying the crew's failure to monitor "may be more the fault of the instruments than the crew".

The choice between this architecture and Mentour's depends on what the public record contains. If the root cause was known within days, a detective structure has to invent suspects, and the notes warn against doing that. If investigators, journalists or the company really did chase wrong leads in public, ACI's suspect loop is honest and gives the video its tension.

## Megaproject and thesis videos both pay the title at about 60%

**The B1M** builds every video on stakes and scale. A 5–9% hook gives a claim, one scale number and an open question, or opens on location. Then come 15–25% of context or history, **a sponsor mid-roll at 22.6–24.3% in four of five videos (80–107 seconds)**, and a long middle that alternates engineering with problems and cost. A controversy or precedent block sits at 50–80%, followed by a conditional future, a short take and an end tag ([Sphere](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Um2Y01tdj7s), [Tegel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d-LArPe1Rk0), [New Orleans](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zEgrugE_eq8), [Airship hangars](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tmte0qRKF1A)). The title's promise arrives late: "on time and on budget" at 65% of Park Avenue ("running almost a hundred million dollars under budget and more than four years ahead of schedule"), Tegel's grenade loop at 64%, New Orleans' cost escalation at 59%. The B1M pays loops by echo, replaying the planted words or footage. Tegel's grenade exchange from 1:17 is replayed nearly word for word at 14:58. The blimp over the skyline at 0:03 is explained at 15:26. Scale comes from **one comparison per beat**, tied to something the viewer already knows. Examples are "a football pitch's worth of land to the sea every 90 minutes", 130 tennis courts, a $1.7 billion Sphere against the $2.5 billion Vegas one, and New Orleans' $48 million relocation against Indonesia's $35 billion capital. Technical points are built as calendar sequences ("First, on a Friday evening… Then, throughout the night… By Saturday afternoon…"), and the constraint comes first, so the engineering reads as the answer to a puzzle. The narrator's opinion is hedged and saved for the last 8–10% of the video, often as a feeling about the place ("It's pretty saddening").

**India in Pixels** reaches the same 60% payoff through argument rather than spectacle. The opening 5% is a claim or joke. Then come a personal confession, a credibility stack (authority quotes, objection handling such as "A lot of people ask a valid question… I hear y'all") and a roadmap sentence. The body is numbered reasons or a map tour (30–50%), each mini-unit built as contrast pairs that add up to a rule. In the Telugu numerals passage, *dwi/do/dui* against *rendu/irandu* leads to "thus we infer the core of Telugu is principally Dravidian" ([Telugu](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GFoj0vG_vs4)). The hook's question is answered at **61% (Mahabharata) and 81% (Telugu)**, with a short delay line about a minute before the answer ("Now before I answer that question let's take a look at some of these complex kingdoms") ([Mahabharata](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0FuotmpYKDg)). The take is flagged as opinion ("if you ask me for my own analysis, let me explain") at a fixed late position of 86–90%, and is followed by a personal close. There is no sponsor read. Every quote here comes from YouTube's English auto-translation of Hindi/Hinglish audio, so the structure is reliable and the wording isn't.

For "how big system X works", combine the two. B1M's order (scale hook, context, constraint first, engineering, cost, precedent, conditional future) gives the tension. IIP's numbered-reasons body (three mechanisms, each a contrast pair) carries the explanation where the B1M would use on-site footage you don't have. IIP's flagged take matches your voice guide's "take a side" rule better than B1M's hedged feelings.

## Timeline scenarios trade explanation for a ticking clock

**The Infographics Show** spends 65–92% of runtime in scenario narration, paced by time markers. There are 60 in "First 60 Minutes if the Internet Goes Down", about one every 9.6 seconds, and 14 log-spaced markers from "600 billionths of a second" to "5 years" in the all-nukes video ([Internet](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=26MgTCz7GhA), [Nukes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zSjopdnJtHc)). Each marker states a consequence of an earlier one ("Minute 13: Due to power reserves being depleted the electricity goes off"), so the cascade does the work that "but" does in a problem chain. Questions are rare: 0 to 5 per video, against 5 to 34 for Veritasium. Escalation tags ("And things are about to get even worse for them and everyone else on the planet") and one or two tonal-contrast beats reset attention. Examples are people stargazing at the Milky Way in a global blackout, and "Internet Going Away parties" ([Electricity](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sSgT5p8dN80)). Mechanism is mostly one clause per minute. Only the nuclear video has a real block (18%), and it is written as a countdown rather than a lecture. Each video closes on a reality check ("Theoretically, it is possible but incredibly unlikely") or a moral, then the same end card. At **203–216 words a minute**, an Infographics script carries about 25–30% more words than a Veritasium script of the same length.

The same premise, built two ways, shows the trade. Kurzgesagt runs every-nuke-at-once as two escalating scenarios in 7:06 and closes with "The planet, itself, doesn't care at all". Infographics runs one 20-minute timeline with a mechanism block in the middle and spends 68 seconds on the same "resilient planet" conclusion ([Kurzgesagt](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JyECrGp-Sw8), [Infographics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zSjopdnJtHc)). For a tech channel, the Infographics shape has a structural gap: there is no room for a mechanism of any length, so a CDN or cloud-region premise shrinks to one minute. Your "what if X failed" template has to put back a primer and a reality check, and those are what would set it apart from the format's many imitators.

## Segmented analysis and news queues pace with chapters, not suspense

**The Culture Mafia** turned out not to be an Indian or tech channel. It is an English-language, **Australia-listed** channel with about 318,000 subscribers that analyses *The Godfather* films and Puzo's novel for "power" lessons ([channel](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCj-qDfDxjiNn62MFRTJTF3A/videos), [IMDb](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt33502033/)). The `hi` caption track is YouTube's auto-translation of the English narration. Use it as a structural model only. Its four sampled videos share a skeleton. A cold-open question or "hidden secret" promise (3–6%) is followed by an in-character like-and-subscribe ritual (~1%), 6–33% of context and a scene replay. The analysis follows (35–55%), cut into labelled steps that are each generalised by an authority quote (Sun Tzu, Machiavelli, Greene's *48 Laws*). There is a mid-video re-hook at 43–53%, then a verdict in the narrator's voice, numbered lessons (8–15%) and an email-list CTA ([Moe Greene](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CAfM4utKKL0), [Fanucci](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kt6HyGAv7QE)). The newest videos formalise this into a **scorecard**: six rounds with a spoken running tally, a draw, then a tie-breaker that becomes the reveal ([Tom Hagen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L7dcIUR3Ayc)). That is the one piece that transfers directly to a tech comparison video such as Postgres against MongoDB, or Kafka against a database table. The integrated sponsor read sits at 62%, bridged from the word "language" for a language-learning app. What doesn't transfer is the fandom demand for an iconic film, the clip-led visuals and the parasocial authority ("A few years ago, I flew to Italy").

**Marcus House** and Manley's **Deep Space Updates** are news queues. House runs a 30–45 second teaser and an ident, then a lead story told as play-by-play (40–66%). A sponsor read lands at **about 10:00 of absolute time in all four videos** (35–43% of runtime), entered on a tease ("I'll come right back to that") and exited with "Ok, getting back to…". Three or four further stories follow ([Flight 13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hF3CHBq56DA), [BZvo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BZvoMn0-JCs)). Manley's roundup is a dated launch ledger (16%), a lead story (25%) and more than half of runtime in short segments linked by "speaking of" ([DSU Nov 3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GzlnPsXOHgw)). The earlier Manley and House report already advised against the roundup format for a new concept channel. The new evidence doesn't change that. One device is worth taking: House places the sponsor read on the episode's biggest tease ("twenty full Version 3 Starlink satellites on board for the first time"), so the read sits on a short open loop.

## Five beat sheets for the channel, with a worked outline each

These five extend, rather than replace, the 8–10 minute misconception-first skeleton and its two alternatives in the [Tech explainer YouTube playbook](Tech%20explainer%20YouTube%20playbook.md). Packaging, the production pipeline and the storyboard table are covered there and in the [Manley and House playbook](YouTube%20playbook%20Manley%20and%20House.md). Runtimes assume your voice guide's 150 words a minute. Shares are a blend of the bands above, adjusted for a faceless channel with no host trust yet. Every incident fact, date and number in the worked outlines is a **placeholder in square brackets to source** before scripting. Where a value is given, it is unverified recollection, included only to show what goes in the slot.

| Topic type | Template | Borrowed from | Use when | Don't use when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Concept explainer | A. Problem chain | Veritasium, Kurzgesagt, Manley's point-building | The concept is a sequence of designs, each fixing the last one's failure | One mechanism with no failure history (use the playbook's build-up explainer) |
| Incident post-mortem, cause known | B. Primer-before-failure | Mentour, Manley's Chernobyl | A clear root cause published in a report or root-cause analysis (RCA) | The cause was disputed or hunted in public |
| Incident post-mortem, cause hunted | C. Detective | ACI | Public record shows real wrong leads and a reconstruction | You'd have to invent suspects |
| "How big system X works" | D. Scale tension with numbered mechanisms | B1M order, IIP reasons, Manley numbers | A large, costly or fragile system with a puzzle-like constraint | Small systems with no scale or cost story |
| "What if X failed" | E. Timeline with a reality check | Infographics, Kurzgesagt ladder | A dependency whose failure cascades in time | The cascade is unknowable, so the timeline would be fiction |

A comparison or verdict video ("Postgres or MongoDB?") is a sixth case. Run it as Template D's body, swapping numbered mechanisms for Culture Mafia scorecard rounds, and end with IIP's flagged take.

## Earlier claims this teardown corrects

Several numbers in the earlier reports and notes came from small or noisy samples, and the full-video reads revise them. The **B1M's main sponsor read is a mid-roll at about 23%** (22.6–24.3% in four of five videos, 80–107 seconds long), with Park Avenue's integrated at 51.5%. The earlier note's "~97% for 5 of 6 videos" described only the short end tag ([Sphere](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Um2Y01tdj7s), [Airship hangars](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Tmte0qRKF1A)). Tegel's mid-roll starts at 24.3%, not about 31%. Pace was overstated. **Manley runs 151–176 wpm** (mean about 160, against the earlier ~170), and **House runs 175–186 wpm** (mean about 178, against the earlier ~205). The earlier House figure counted 5,462 words for Flight 12, and a de-duplicated count gives about 4,730. A word-tag cross-check supports the lower number, so rolling caption lines were most likely duplicated ([Flight 12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aPtMGJvJ72g)). The playbook's statement that House talks at 205 wpm should now read 178. These are runtime averages that include music and pauses, so treat them as ±5%.

Smaller corrections follow. House's "fixed Apollo-style ident" is a family of idents: absent in one video and reworded in another. Both creators' sign-offs are families of phrases, not single lines. The captions limit what any of this can prove. Manley, House, Mentour, two of the three ACI uploads, Kurzgesagt's 2024 video and The Culture Mafia are YouTube auto-captions. Timestamps carry ±5 to ±30 seconds of error depending on the source. Samples are 3–5 videos per channel. **No retention data was available**, so these beat sheets describe what the scripts do, not what holds viewers.

## Conclusion

Picking a channel to imitate matters less than picking the right architecture for each topic. Your four topic types map onto four different clocks. A concept explainer runs on a chain of broken designs. A post-mortem runs on dramatic irony (primer-before-failure) or on mystery (detective), and the public record decides which is honest. A system tour runs on a cost or scale tension paid at about 60%. A what-if runs on a literal clock. Rotating five architectures also answers the risk the earlier playbook raised: YouTube's inauthentic-content policy targets "templated storylines" that feel "interchangeable from video to video". A channel whose videos each have a different spine, and whose transitions come from one voice, is less exposed than one that pours every topic into the same skeleton.

Two departures from the source channels are deliberate and should be tested against your own retention graphs. First, every template here explains more than the channel it borrows from. Template E adds a primer and a reality check that Infographics lacks, and Template D swaps the B1M's on-site footage for numbered mechanisms. A faceless AI channel can't trade on access or a host's face, so the explanation has to be the product. Second, the templates pull the payoff earlier than the long-form originals do. Mentour's event at 65–79% and ACI's answer at about 90% are earned by audiences who already trust the host or the brand. A new channel should start the event at about 50–55% and close the main loop by about 85%, then move those points later only if retention shows viewers will wait.
