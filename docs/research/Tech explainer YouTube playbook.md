# Make tech explainers people finish watching

A good long-form tech explainer is mostly decided before production starts. Pick an idea you can package. Open on a concrete question or a wrong belief within about ten seconds. Correct that belief from a concrete example up to the abstract idea, write it to be heard at about 150 words a minute, and time cheap storyboard frames to the voiceover before you generate anything expensive. The strongest research-backed lever is Derek Muller's: a video that voices the viewer's misconception and then breaks it nearly doubled post-test scores (6.3 to about 11 out of 26), while a clear, correct explanation taught almost nothing. Production is now cheap. An 8-minute video built from AI stills, a little AI video, a cloned narrator and code-driven animation costs roughly **$10–35 in marginal API spend** and about **$30–100 a month** in fixed tools. The work that takes time is research, script editing and checking the visuals. The binding constraint for a faceless AI channel is YouTube's July 2025 "inauthentic content" policy. It does not penalise AI use. It penalises videos that feel "interchangeable from video to video", so every episode needs its own angle, its own visual structure and your editorial voice. This report sets out what the evidence supports, flags what is only folklore, and ends with a reusable pipeline, script skeleton and storyboard table.

## Packaging comes first because the recommender predicts satisfaction, not clicks

YouTube's stated model is a per-viewer prediction of satisfaction. Todd Beaupré described the homepage as "pulling" what each viewer will enjoy, measured through clicks and watch time and also through "likes, dislikes, these survey responses", and he advised judging a video over **90 days or more** because old videos resurface when interest returns ([SEJ](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/how-youtubes-recommendation-system-works-in-2025/538379/)). YouTube publishes no weights. Treat claims like "a share is worth 5–8 likes" as folklore. One mechanism is official and very useful: **Test & Compare** lets you test up to three titles and/or thumbnails, and it picks the winner by **watch time share, not CTR**. Tests finish within two weeks, and editing the title or thumbnail mid-test stops the test ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13861714)). The test therefore rewards packaging that delivers on its promise. A misleading variant can win clicks and still lose.

That is why creators settle the idea, title and thumbnail before they write a script. Paddy Galloway's rule is that if packaging cannot make an idea clickable, it is not an idea. He also says to study outliers in your niche, make about three thumbnails per video, and write a prediction before upload so you can later tell whether the idea, the packaging or the content failed ([Startup Ideas Podcast](https://share.transistor.fm/s/d34a7e0e); [summary](https://www.skills.sh/getedgehq/skills/galloway-youtube-outlier-packaging)). He claims **90%+ of growth comes from one idea** ([thread](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1874513765762531734.html)). That is anecdote, but it argues for heavy ideation over heavy polishing. Muller's version of the ethics is "legitbait": a title that grabs attention and is true. He contrasts it with titles that mislead and cause backlash, and he warns that a title only insiders understand caps reach ([Gigazine on Veritasium](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20210830-clickbait-effective/)). Justin Kuiper, who writes for large channels, calls the gap between what the package promises and what the video delivers the "promise delta", and says it should be "as close to zero as possible" ([Human Invariant](https://www.humaninvariant.com/blog/youtube-interview)).

A teardown of 27 famous explainers shows what the packaging looks like in practice. Titles are short, with a **median of 41 characters and 6 words**. They usually make a claim or pose a puzzle ("The Biggest Misconception About Electricity", "What if We Nuke the Moon?", "Why Budget Airlines are Suddenly Failing") rather than label a topic. Outside Fireship, the titles rarely contain numbers. Every thumbnail viewed carried **0–4 words**. The common pairing is a title that makes a claim and a thumbnail that asks a question or adds a new angle. Veritasium titled a video "Biggest Misconception About Electricity" and put "ENERGY DOESN'T FLOW IN WIRES" on the thumbnail ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bHIhgxav9LY)). Wendover's "Suddenly Failing" carries "WHAT WENT WRONG?" ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4-Pu3CXatsA)). The Infographics Show's "Minute By Minute" carries "WOULD YOU SURVIVE?" ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qUxZ4T6IMIM)). Most science and tech thumbnails show one object or diagram, not a face. They use dark backgrounds with saturated glow, or flat bright colour, plus at most one device: a red arrow, a single "?", or a number badge such as Branch Education's "12 TB" ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wtdnatmVdIg)). A faceless channel loses little by having no face in the thumbnail.

Plan on 8–12 minutes. Mid-roll ads need **8+ minutes** ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-8-minute-mid-roll-ads/)). The monetization gap between niches comes mostly from how long viewers stay. AIR's dataset of 300 channels puts **Education & Science at a median RPM of $10.22**, with about 77% of views served an ad. **Gadgets & Tech sits at $2.33** with only 34%, because early exits leave views unpaid ([AIR](https://air.io/en/air-data-findings/which-youtube-niche-makes-the-most-money-in-2026-ranked-by-real-rpm-and-cpm)). A tech-concepts channel will land between those two figures depending on its retention, so budget conservatively at **$3–8 RPM**. It earns nothing until it reaches the YouTube Partner Program threshold of 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 watch hours ([kingy.ai](https://kingy.ai/news/how-to-make-money-using-ai-on-youtube-in-2026-the-complete-guide/)). Never pad a video to reach eight minutes. A high-CTR, low-watch-time video reads as clickbait.

## The first ten seconds hold a question, a number or a wrong belief, never a welcome

Every source agrees that the first minute is where most viewers are lost. Vendor benchmarks say **over 55% of viewers leave in the first minute**. They put average retention at 23.7% overall and about 31.5% for 5–10 minute videos ([Stripo, relaying Retention Rabbit](https://research.stripo.email/youtube-benchmarks)), and vidIQ reports 30.3% across its own 16.5M long-form views ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/increase-audience-retention-youtube/)). Blogs that call 40–60% "good" are probably describing Shorts. Compare yourself with Studio's "typical" curve instead. The leaked MrBeast production guide, whose authenticity is disputed, calls minute one "the most important minute". It also advises 3-minute segments, a new storyline whenever the current one slows, and the rule that "the ending has to hit" ([Chewning](https://zackchewning.substack.com/p/content-lessons-from-the-1-youtubers); [Creator Handbook](https://www.creatorhandbook.net/leaked-document-allegedly-reveals-mrbeasts-secrets-to-youtube-success-the-key-takeaways/)).

The 27 openers show what works in practice. **Seven state their core question within about 10 seconds.** The ones that wait longer fill the gap with a concrete image, a number or a paradox, never with background. Only an old ColdFusion episode opens with "Hi, welcome to…" ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LoTx9LQIKEA)). Two channels show the craft shifting over time. Kurzgesagt went from a definition-first 2014 Big Bang opener, which reached its question at 56 seconds ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wNDGgL73ihY)), to "Is the universe infinite?" at 5 seconds in 2024 ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=isdLel273rQ)). Branch Education's 2018 touchscreen video wandered for 41 seconds ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cFvh7qM6LdA)). Its 2024 microchip video opens: "Inside this smartphone are 62 microchips containing a total of 90 billion transistors… how are billions of nanoscopic transistors manufactured into a microchip the size of a tiny ant?" ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dX9CGRZwD-w)). The strong hooks withhold something specific. Veritasium withholds the answer ("I want you to commit to an answer… when I tell you the answer later on"). Johnny Harris withholds what the building is. The math video withholds the axiom's name.

| Hook template | Formula for the first 30 seconds | Real example | Best for tech concepts when… |
|---|---|---|---|

| Confessed misconception | "I believed X." Surprising truth, then "I'll prove it", then a thought experiment | Veritasium quantum paths ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qJZ1Ez28C-A)) | The audience holds a common wrong model |

These templates come from one sample chosen by channel reputation. No one has ranked them against a matched control, so A/B them against your own retention curves. Two caveats come from the data. Sponsor-first openers still reached 28M and 4.9M views, so a short sponsor line is not fatal. And 3Blue1Brown's Fourier video, which opens with a roadmap and no question, worked because its title and thumbnail already carried the promise ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=spUNpyF58BY)).

## Misconception-first structure nearly doubles learning, and it feels harder to watch

The best-evidenced structural rule comes from Muller's PhD research on physics films. Students who watched a clear, correct explanation scored **6.0 before and 6.3 after, out of 26**. They rated it clear and came away *more* confident in their misconceptions. A version in which an actor voiced the common wrong ideas, followed by a dialogue on why they fail, raised scores to **about 11 out of 26**. Students called that version more confusing and reported more mental effort ([Muller](https://rosetta.to/u/veritasium/khan-academy-and-the-effectiveness-of-science-videos)). His prescription is to engage prior knowledge, or "give people an interesting conundrum", so viewers "come along with you on that journey" ([Creator Handbook interview](https://www.creatorhandbook.net/inside-veritasium-an-interview-with-dr-derek-muller-12ad2351a4b3/)). For tech concepts this means naming the plausible wrong model first: "the cloud is someone else's computer somewhere", "encryption scrambles the message so nobody can read it", "more RAM makes everything faster". Make the viewer commit to it, then break it with a running example. Don't tune the script for "that was so clear" comments. Muller's data show that feeling and learning come apart.

Grant Sanderson supplies the second rule: **go from the concrete to the abstract**. Show several similar examples so the viewer sees the pattern before you name it. Motivate each definition so the learner feels they could have discovered it. He admits his own first drafts slip back into textbook order, with the abstraction first, and have to be rewritten. He also warns that visuals which look good but don't explain are a mistake ([3Blue1Brown](https://rosetta.to/u/3blue1brown/make-math-videos-summer-of-math-exposition-announcement)). For software, that means one request, one packet or one function call traced end to end before any architecture diagram appears. Kurzgesagt calls the script "the backbone of our videos". Its scripts go through **about a dozen drafts**, built on primary sources, expert fact-checks and a published sourcesheet per video ([Kurzgesagt](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)). The team calls writing "the bottleneck… very hard to speed up" ([Kurzgesagt, 1200 hours](https://rosetta.to/u/kurzgesagt/how-to-make-a-kurzgesagt-video-in-1200-hours)). Sanderson tests sample lessons on a friend ([AMS Notices](https://www.ams.org/notices/202210/rnoti-p1789.pdf)).

Writing for the ear starts with arithmetic. Speech runs at **about 150 words per minute**, against 200–300 for reading, and narration can be leaner than an essay because music and visuals carry the tone ([Human Invariant](https://www.humaninvariant.com/blog/youtube-interview)). An 8–10 minute video is therefore about **1,200–1,500 words**. Aim lower for dense material, because Sanderson regrets the "fire hose rate" of his first video ([3Blue1Brown](https://rosetta.to/u/3blue1brown/make-math-videos-summer-of-math-exposition-announcement)). Johnny Harris alternates "feeling" and "thinking" scenes ([Rosetta](https://rosetta.to/u/johnnyharris/topic/content-creation)). Combined with the MrBeast guide's 3-minute segments, this suggests a re-hook (a new question, reversal or "but") every 2–3 minutes. That interval and the but/therefore beat (borrowed from South Park's writers, not from any YouTube creator) are craft conventions without primary evidence, so confirm them against your own retention dips. The script stage is where your own voice goes in. Apply the style guide at `/Users/psbakre/Documents/youtube-channel/my-writing-style.md` when you edit Claude's draft, so the narration sounds like one person with opinions rather than a template. That matters for craft, and it also matters for policy (see below).

## The storyboard is timed to the voiceover, then built from a fixed visual vocabulary

Kurzgesagt's published order is research, script, a sketching phase to work out visual metaphors, illustration, animation timed to the narrator's voiceover, and finally custom music and SFX ([Kurzgesagt](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)). A 2020 breakdown put one ~9-minute video at **~1,200 hours**, with about 200 sketches and 8–12 weeks each of illustration and animation for 2–3 people per stage. Narration was recorded paragraph by paragraph and SFX came last ([GIGAZINE](https://www.gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20200217-science-animation-making/)). Copy the order, not the hours. Animation practice explains why voiceover comes first: re-timing keyframes after a re-record can cost more than making the video ([Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2019/07/15/animation-editing/)). In an AI pipeline, lock the script, generate the voiceover (even a scratch take), and pull the word timestamps. Then lay out cheap storyboard frames against those timestamps before spending on images or video. No primary process documentation exists for The Infographics Show. An outside analysis calls its style systematic and repeatable ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/channel/theinfographicsshow)), which matches what its videos look like but proves nothing about its tools.

For animated explainers the storyboard is the working document. Each row pairs a voiceover line with the visual, the on-screen text and the timing; a camera-style shot list is for live action ([Pexo](https://pexo.ai/blog/explainer-video-storyboard-1513); [Knowlify](https://knowlify.com/articles/animated-video-storyboarding-guide)). AI-era shot lists add a prompt, the assets and a review status for each shot ([Magic Hour](https://magichour.ai/blog/ai-video-shot-list-template)). Sanderson's rule decides what goes in the visual column. The visual must show exactly what the narration describes and be part of the story. If an idea won't animate, change the approach rather than decorate it ([iaps.info](https://iaps.info/2025/01/22/exploring-the-path-of-3blue1brown-a-conversation-with-grant-sanderson/)). The working grammar below is practitioner convention, not sourced research. Build diagrams one element per narrated clause, and dim earlier elements instead of removing them. Give each system one metaphor and never swap it: a server is always the same icon, a request always the same token. Give each concept one colour for the whole video, with no more than 3–4 accents, and pair colour with shape for colour-blind viewers. Move between zoom levels in the same order (system, then component, then detail, then back out). Highlight only the code lines being discussed, keep code to at most 10–12 lines, and use diff-style transitions. Reserve kinetic type for one key term or number at a time.

On pacing, the oft-repeated "new visual every 3–4 seconds" rule has no rigorous source behind it ([Fluxnote](https://fluxnote.io/guides/faceless-shorts-optimal-length-2026)). A workable reading is a new visual *state* every 3–6 seconds, with small motion during longer holds, checked against your retention graph. Kurzgesagt treats sound as part of the explanation: an original score for each video and SFX as the final pass ([Kurzgesagt](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)). For a solo channel that means SFX on each build-up beat, music ducked under the narration, and a music change at each section boundary. Consistency matters twice for an AI channel. It makes the channel recognisable, and it reduces model drift between generations. Kurzgesagt keeps a strict house style of flat 2D with 3D elements, "quirky and relatable characters" and one narrator in every video ([Kurzgesagt](https://kurzgesagt.org/youtube/)). Your equivalent is a one-page style bible: palette hex codes with a role for each colour, line and shading rules, 3–5 recurring characters or icons with reference images, font specs, motion easing, and a fixed prompt prefix plus reference-image set used for every generation.

## An AI pipeline costs about $10–35 per video, and the policy line is "interchangeable"

**Voice** is the cheapest item on the bill, so spend quality there. ElevenLabs v3 runs about **$0.08–0.10 per 1,000 characters**, roughly $5 per narrated hour. MiniMax and OpenAI's gpt-4o-mini-tts (about $0.015 a minute, with requests capped at 2,000 tokens so long scripts must be chunked) are cheaper, and Kokoro runs locally for free ([invideo.io](https://invideo.io/blog/ai-voice-model-pricing/); [OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-4o-mini-tts); [manim-skill](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/yusuke710/manim-skill@main/README.md)). An 8-minute script is about 7,000 characters, or **under $1 on ElevenLabs**. Cloning your own voice is the strategic choice. YouTube explicitly exempts "cloning your own voice for voiceovers or dubs" from disclosure ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)). It also separates you from stock-AI-voice channels. Add a pronunciation dictionary for technical terms.

**Visuals** split three ways. Still images cost $0.039–0.134 each on the Nano Banana family, and Nano Banana 2 costs $0.067 at 1K ([benchlm](https://benchlm.ai/media-pricing/nano-banana)). FLUX 2 pro costs about $0.03 per megapixel ([apiframe](https://apiframe.ai/blog/ai-image-api-pricing-2026)). AI video clips are the expensive part, but the cheap tiers are now viable for short abstract B-roll: **Veo 3.1 Lite at $0.05/s**, Kling 3.0 Turbo at about $0.11/s, and full Veo 3.1 at $0.40/s ([invideo.io](https://invideo.io/blog/ai-video-model-pricing)). An aggregator reports that OpenAI's Sora API was scheduled to shut down on 24 September 2026, which is unconfirmed, so don't build on it. For tech explainers the highest-value visual layer is code-driven animation that Claude can write. **Manim** (3Blue1Brown's Python engine) suits algorithms, data structures and step-by-step diagrams ([3Blue1Brown](https://www.3blue1brown.com/lessons/manim-demo/)). **Remotion** (React) suits UI-style motion graphics, code diffs, captions and overall assembly. Remotion shipped official Claude agent skills in January 2026 (`npx skills add remotion-dev/skills`) and is free for individuals and companies of up to three people ([VloggingPro](https://vloggingpro.com/ai/workflows/agentic-remotion-videos/); [Remotion license](https://remotion.dev/docs/license)). Open-source Claude Code skills already chain plan, code, render and iterate for Manim, including yusuke710/manim-skill and NousResearch's manim-video ([hermes manim-video](https://openskillindex.com/skills/nousresearch-hermes-agent-manim-video-478ff0)). One developer has documented a zero-cost Claude, Manim, local TTS and FFmpeg pipeline ([amineelfarssi](https://amineelfarssi.github.io/blog/youtube-channel-zero-cost-pipeline/)). Quality comes from the visual QA loop: render frames, have Claude inspect them, fix and re-render.

**Music** should come from a licensed library. Epidemic Sound starts at $9.99/month, and its tracks are reportedly cleared for linked channels. The YouTube Audio Library is free ([creatorstackclub](https://www.creatorstackclub.com/software/epidemic-sound)). AI music from Suno needs a paid tier ($10/month and up) for commercial rights and still carries Content ID and label-litigation uncertainty ([Dubspot](https://blog.dubspot.com/ai-music-licensing-explained-2026)). Captions should come from your own script plus the TTS timestamps, not from speech recognition, so technical terms are never mangled.

The time estimate is a judgement: **6–12 hours per video** while the pipeline is new. Most of it goes to research and fact-checking (2–3 h), script editing (1–2 h), visual QA (2–4 h) and packaging and upload (about 1 h). Manim and Remotion debugging is the main source of variance. No independent case study of an AI-made tech-explainer channel with real analytics exists. Treat "$X a month with AI" claims as marketing.

**Policy** is the real gate. On **15 July 2025** YouTube renamed "repetitious content" to **"inauthentic content"**. The disallowed examples include content that "looks like it's made with a template", that "feels interchangeable from video to video", "image slideshows, templated storylines, or scrolling text with minimal or no narrative", and "AI-generated content made with generic or unoriginal templates giving the impression of mass production". The same page explicitly allows automated tools as long as "the final product must still demonstrate your creative vision", and it lists AI script editing and unique AI background visuals as acceptable ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392)). YouTube described the change as a clarification, not a ban on AI ([exchange4media](https://www.exchange4media.com/digital-news/youtube-issues-clarification-on-monetisation-policy-update-after-creator-backlash-145327.html)). Enforcement reports are blog-sourced. They describe channel-level review, terminations of large faceless channels in January 2026, and weak formats such as AI slideshows, generic top-10s and template clones ([letsdatascience](https://letsdatascience.com/news/youtube-cracks-down-on-faceless-creators-monetization-e3bb5892); [AIR](https://air.io/en/news-air/how-youtube-treats-ai-in-2026-new-update)). **Disclosure** is a separate rule and a narrower one. The "AI use" toggle is required only for *realistic* synthetic content: a real person shown doing what they didn't, altered real footage, a realistic event that never happened, or AI music as the main focus. AI scripts, thumbnails, infographics, animated or non-realistic visuals, captions and your own cloned voice are exempt, and disclosing does not limit reach or monetization ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491)). A stylised, diagram-led channel narrated by your own voice is therefore on the safe side of both rules, provided each video carries an original angle and an original visual structure.

## The operating playbook: pipeline, script skeleton and storyboard

The pipeline below follows the evidence above: packaging before script, misconception-first and concrete-to-abstract structure, voiceover before visuals, cheap frames before expensive ones, and human judgement at each gate the inauthentic-content policy cares about.

| Stage | Who / tool | What you produce | Gate before moving on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Idea and outlier scan | You + Claude (web research) | 10 candidate concepts; outliers from small and mid-size channels in the niche | Is there a common wrong belief or a surprising number? |
| 2. Packaging first | Claude drafts, you choose | 3 titles (claim/puzzle, ≤~45 chars), 3 thumbnail concepts (≤4 words, different angle from the title), a written performance prediction | Would you click it? Can the video fully deliver the promise? If not, kill the idea |
| 3. Research and source sheet | Claude with primary sources | Source sheet: every claim, number and citation; recalculated figures | Every number traces to a primary source |
| 4. Outline | Claude | Beat outline on the script skeleton below; misconception named; one running concrete example chosen | Concrete example appears before any abstraction |
| 5. Draft | Claude | ~1,100–1,400 words | — |
| 6. Voice edit | You | Rewrite in your voice per `my-writing-style.md`; add your own opinion or insight and one original element (benchmark, demo, original diagram) | Does it sound like you, and could it only be this video? |
| 7. Ear pass and test | You (read aloud), one non-expert listener | Shorter sentences, spoken numbers, re-hook every 2–3 min, a "but/therefore" at each turn | Listener can restate the idea and the misconception |
| 8. Fact-check | Claude against the source sheet, then you | Corrected script, locked | Nothing unverified ships |
| 9. Voiceover | ElevenLabs (own cloned voice + pronunciation dictionary), paragraph by paragraph | Audio + word timestamps | Re-take any mispronunciation now; the timing is fixed after this |
| 10. Storyboard | Claude from script + timestamps | Storyboard table below, one row per beat, durations from timestamps | Every row explains something rather than decorates; one metaphor per system |
| 11. Animatic | Remotion with placeholder frames | Rough cut on the voiceover timeline | Watch it at phone size; fix pacing gaps before generating assets |
| 12. Asset generation | Manim/Remotion code (Claude); Nano Banana for stills; Veo Lite/Kling for B-roll | Assets with IDs, all using the style-bible prompt prefix and reference images | Render frames; Claude and you inspect; regenerate drift |
| 13. Sound | Epidemic/YouTube Audio Library, SFX pack | Music bed ducked under VO, section changes, SFX on build beats | No Content ID-risky tracks |
| 14. Assembly and captions | Remotion/ffmpeg | Final render; SRT from script + timestamps | Full watch at 1x on a phone |
| 15. Upload | YouTube Studio | Title/thumbnail Test & Compare (3 variants); chapters; source sheet linked; "AI use" toggled only if anything photorealistic appears | — |
| 16. Review | You | At 48 h, 2 weeks and 90 days: check CTR and retention dips against the prediction; label the failure as idea, packaging or content | Feed the lessons into the next video's stages 2, 4 and 10 |

**Script skeleton (misconception-first, 8–10 min, ~1,200–1,500 words).** The timings are a starting hypothesis. Adjust them from your retention graph.

| Time | Beat | Job | Writing rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:10 | Hook | Concrete question, number, or wrong belief (choose a template from the hook table) | No welcome, no channel name; withhold one specific thing |
| 0:10–0:30 | Promise | Restate the title's promise; raise the stakes ("this is why X breaks") | Match the thumbnail's angle; keep the promise delta at zero |
| 0:30–1:30 | Setup | One concrete scenario the viewer recognises; make them predict | "Commit to an answer" moment |
| 1:30–3:30 | Break the belief | Run the example; show exactly where the intuitive model fails | Voice the misconception fairly before breaking it (Muller) |
| ~3:00 | Re-hook 1 | A "but" reversal or new question | Open a new loop before the old one closes |
| 3:30–6:30 | Build the right model | 2–3 steps, each one named mechanism, concrete then general | Motivate every definition; one idea per sentence |
| ~6:00 | Re-hook 2 | Twist, edge case, or real-world failure | Feeling beat between thinking beats |
| 6:30–8:00 | Test it | Apply the model to a new case; viewer predicts first | Prove the model, don't just restate it |
| 8:00–9:00 | Payoff | Return to the opening scenario; resolve the withheld item; one-line takeaway | "The ending has to hit"; no recap list |
| last 10 s | Exit | Point to the next video on a related misconception | No long outro; end on content |

Two alternatives keep the format from becoming a template. The first is the **question journey**: ask the question, give the obvious answer and show why it fails, give a better answer and show why it still fails, then reveal the real answer and its consequences, and finally zoom out. The second is the **build-up explainer**: show the finished system working, build the smallest version, add a constraint that breaks it, add the next component, repeat, show the full diagram only once every part is known, and close on the opening demo. Rotating between the three skeletons, with differently structured visuals each time, is your defence against "interchangeable from video to video".

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. Production, once the 1,200-hour moat behind a single Kurzgesagt video, now costs about the price of lunch per video. Research rigour, the choice of misconception and packaging judgement have not got cheaper, and that is where competitors are also weakest. The same three things are what YouTube's inauthentic-content policy looks for, so the channel's quality strategy and its policy strategy are the same strategy. The open questions are empirical: which hook template, re-hook interval and visual pacing suit *your* audience. No source settles them. Treat the first 20–30 videos as instrumented experiments, each with a written prediction, a Test & Compare run and a 90-day retention review. Expect, as Galloway's data suggest, that one idea will drive most of the early growth, so iterate on ideas and packaging much harder than on polish.
